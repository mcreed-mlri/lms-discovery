# Critical incident interview guide

A guide for collecting **critical incidents**: specific, real moments where a new legal aid advocate's actions clearly helped or hurt the outcome. The incidents become the practice tasks, difficulty levels, feedback rubrics and scenario details described in `docs/learning-design.md`.

**This works alongside the pilot interview already sent to staff.** Pilot answers can be coded with the same sheet (part 4). Follow-up interviews fill the gaps the pilot leaves.

## 1. Before you start

**Who to interview**

- **Experienced attorneys and supervisors:** they have seen many new advocates handle the same situations. Most of your incidents come from them.
- **Attorneys in their first two years:** about their own recent moments. They remember what was confusing and what they wish they had known.
- **Judges, clerks or court staff** if anyone has access: what they see new advocates do in court.
- **Non-lawyer advocates and paralegals** for skills they practice, so scenarios fit their role and UPL limits.

**How many**

- Aim for 20 to 40 incidents per skill area, from at least 5 people, roughly half effective and half ineffective.
- Stop when new interviews mostly repeat incidents you already have.

**Format**

- 30 to 45 minutes, one to one, by video or in person. Two to four incidents per interview is typical.
- Take notes on the incident form (part 3). Record only with consent, and delete recordings once notes are written up.

**Confidentiality (read this to every interviewee)**

- Describe the situation, not the client. No names, case numbers, addresses, dates of birth or other identifying facts.
- If a detail could identify someone, change it or leave it out.
- Incidents will be rewritten as fictional scenarios before anyone else sees them.
- Their name will not be attached to any incident.

## 2. The interview

### Opening (2 minutes)

> Thanks for making time. We're building practice exercises for new attorneys, and we want them to be about real situations, not textbook ones. I'll ask you to think of specific moments you've seen, where a new advocate did something that made a real difference, for better or worse. Please leave out anything that could identify a client; change details if you need to. Your name won't be attached to anything. Is it OK if I take notes?

### Warm-up (3 minutes)

1. How long have you been practicing, and in what areas?
2. How often do you work with or supervise attorneys in their first two years?

### Incident questions (repeat for each incident)

Start with an **effective** incident, then an **ineffective** one, then whichever kind they have more of.

**Prompt for an effective incident**

> Think of a specific time a new advocate handled something especially well, where what they did clearly made a difference. It could be in court, with a client, on the phone, or in writing.

**Prompt for an ineffective incident**

> Now think of a specific time a new advocate's actions led to a worse result than they should have, or a near miss. Again, a specific moment, not a general habit.

**For each incident, ask the five core questions:**

1. **What was the situation?** Where were they, what stage was the case at, and what was at stake?
2. **What exactly did they do or say?** As close to their actual words or actions as you can remember.
3. **What happened as a result?**
4. **Why was that effective, or not?**
5. **What would an experienced advocate have done?**

**Follow-up probes** (use the ones that fit):

- What made this situation harder than usual?
- What did they need to know in that moment? Did they know it?
- Was there a step or rule they missed, or a judgment call they got wrong?
- Was there anything the client, the other side or the judge did that took them by surprise?
- Is this something you see often, or was it unusual?
- How do new advocates usually learn to handle this today?

**Keep it specific.** If the answer drifts into generalities ("they should be better prepared"), bring it back: "Can you think of one particular time that happened?"

### Skill-specific prompts (optional)

Use these when collecting incidents for one skill area. Example for hearsay:

- Think of a time a new attorney faced a hearsay objection they didn't expect. What happened?
- Have you seen someone lose evidence that should have come in? What went wrong?
- What do new attorneys most often get confused about with layered hearsay?
- What does a judge typically ask when hearsay comes up at a hearing?

Example for eviction defense, first 48 hours:

- Think of an intake call about a notice to quit where the advocate caught something important. What was it?
- Have you seen a deadline missed or nearly missed? What led to it?
- What do clients often leave out that turns out to matter?

### Closing (3 minutes)

1. Is there a situation you wish new attorneys could practice before facing it for real?
2. Is there anyone else we should talk to?
3. Can we follow up if we have a question about an incident?

> Thank you. Everything will be anonymised and fictionalised before anyone else sees it.

## 3. Incident form

One form per incident. Fill it in during or straight after the interview.

| Field                         | Notes                                                             |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Incident ID                   | e.g. HS-07 (skill area code and number)                           |
| Skill area                    | e.g. Hearsay, eviction intake                                     |
| Effective or ineffective      |                                                                   |
| Setting                       | Court hearing, client meeting, phone, written filing…             |
| Situation                     | What was happening and what was at stake (no identifying details) |
| What the advocate did or said | As specific as possible                                           |
| Outcome                       |                                                                   |
| Why it worked or didn't       | In the interviewee's words                                        |
| What an expert would do       |                                                                   |
| What made it hard             | Complexity factors                                                |
| What they needed to know      |                                                                   |
| Surprises                     | From the client, the other side, the judge                        |

| Interviewee role              | Supervisor, experienced attorney, new attorney… (no name)         |

## 4. Coding sheet: turning incidents into design

After a batch of interviews (and for the pilot answers you already have), code each incident in a spreadsheet with these columns. Each column feeds a part of the 4C/ID design in `docs/learning-design.md`.

| Column                         | Question to answer                                                                    | Becomes                                                    |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Task**                       | What whole task was the advocate doing?                                               | A learning task                                            |
| **Complexity factors**         | What made it harder? (time pressure, layered issues, hostile judge, client in crisis) | Task classes: easy to hard                                 |
| **Effective behaviours**       | What did they do that worked?                                                         | Rubric: what "strong" looks like                           |
| **Ineffective behaviours**     | What went wrong?                                                                      | Rubric: what "weak" looks like; common-mistake feedback    |
| **Judgment needed**            | Where did they have to weigh options?                                                 | Supportive information to teach                            |
| **Steps or rules**             | What fixed procedure applied?                                                         | Procedural information and checklists                      |
| **Needs to be automatic**      | Anything that must be fast and error-free?                                            | Part-task drills                                           |
| **Hidden facts and surprises** | What wasn't obvious at first?                                                         | Scenario hidden facts and escalations                      |
| **Exact words**                | Memorable phrasing from the judge, client or other side                               | Scenario dialogue (fictionalised)                          |
| **Search terms**               | How would an advocate describe this when looking for help?                            | Search benchmark questions and synonyms (`docs/search.md`) |

**Then, for the skill area as a whole:**

1. Group incidents by **task**. The most frequent tasks are the ones to build practice for first.
2. Sort each task's incidents by **complexity factors** into three or four levels. These are the task classes.
3. Collect the **effective and ineffective behaviours** into a draft rubric. Have two experienced attorneys review it.
4. List the **judgment** points and **steps** separately: they become supportive and procedural information.
5. Pick two or three incidents per task class to rewrite as fictional scenarios, using the scenario spec in `docs/simulations.md`.

## 5. Worked example (fictional)

**Incident HS-03, ineffective, court hearing.** At a summary process trial, a new attorney offered a supervisor's declaration that quoted an intake worker's note of what the tenant said. Opposing counsel objected to it as hearsay. The attorney argued that the declaration was a business record, but did not address the tenant's statement inside the note. The judge excluded the note. An experienced attorney would have analysed each layer separately and offered the tenant's statement for notice rather than for its truth. _Hard because:_ two layers, and the attorney was thinking on their feet. _Common:_ "Very. Layered statements trip up almost everyone at first."

**Coded:**

| Column                     | Entry                                                                        |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Task                       | Defend against a hearsay objection                                           |
| Complexity factors         | Layered statement; oral, at a hearing                                        |
| Effective behaviours       | (none in this incident)                                                      |
| Ineffective behaviours     | Addressed only the outer layer                                               |
| Judgment needed            | Choosing the purpose the inner statement is offered for                      |
| Steps or rules             | Analyse each layer separately                                                |
| Needs to be automatic      | Spotting that there are two layers                                           |
| Hidden facts and surprises | The judge focused on the inner statement                                     |
| Search terms               | "layered hearsay", "hearsay within hearsay", "declaration hearsay objection" |

**Becomes:** task class 2 (layered) and class 3 (oral) in the Hearsay blueprint; a rubric point ("analysed each layer"); a "spot the layers" rapid drill; the judge's follow-up question as an escalation in the AI hearing scenario.

## 6. Working with the pilot already sent

- **Code the pilot answers first** with the sheet in part 4. Note which columns are empty or vague.
- **Compare the pilot's questions with the five core questions** (part 2). Answers to general questions ("What do new attorneys struggle with?") rarely give a specific incident. Use follow-up interviews to get specifics behind them.
- **Use the pilot to choose whom to interview:** people whose answers were rich are good follow-up candidates.
- **Keep a single incident list** across the pilot and the interviews, with an ID for each, so nothing is counted twice.
